I have the following idea on combining between dflash2 and ddtree, please rate it

My #1 idea: DDTree × DFlash 2

On August 18, the DFlash authors released DFlash 2.

DFlash 2 specifically addresses the fact that the correct token is often somewhere in DFlash's candidate set but isn't top-1. For their Qwen example, the target token is DFlash's top-1 candidate at the first position about 85.4% of the time, but is inside the top-16 99.5% of the time. Their oracle top-16 selector would move acceptance length from 4.27 → 6.79.

Their solution is extremely interesting for DDTree.

Instead of just giving

$$ q_i(b), $$

DFlash 2 has a tiny learned pairwise path selector that scores:

$$ S_i(a,b) $$

meaning roughly:

given candidate token \(a\) immediately before it, how plausible is candidate \(b\)?

It computes the pairwise scores for the top-16 candidates in parallel, with only about 2M extra parameters and reportedly ~0.6% cycle-latency overhead for the selector itself.

But DFlash 2 then uses those scores to select one path.

DDTree does exactly the opposite:

don't gamble on one path; hedge by verifying several paths.

So there is a very natural synthesis:

Pairwise / Markov DDTree

Instead of DDTree's independent surrogate

$$ Q_{\text{DDTree}}(u) = \prod_i q_i(u_i), $$

construct something like

$$ Q_{\text{pair}}(u) = r_1(u_1\mid b) \prod_{i=2}^{d} r_i(u_i\mid u_{i-1}) $$

where the \(r_i\)'s are derived from DFlash 2's pairwise selector.

Conceptually:

DDTree

DFlash marginals → independent path probabilities → tree

DFlash 2

DFlash candidates → pairwise coherence → ONE path

Your method

DFlash 2 candidates → pairwise coherence → TREE

That combination is extremely natural.

And in the literature/searches I ran, I did not find an existing paper that combines DFlash 2's pairwise selector with DDTree-style multi-branch verification. Given that DFlash 2 was only released August 18, that's unsurprising. I would still do a more exhaustive literature check before making a novelty claim.

There's even a neat theorem hiding here

Something I like a lot about this direction is that DDTree's Proposition 1 is actually more general than the particular independent \(Q\) they use.

Their proof essentially uses

$$ \mathbb E[\alpha_T(Y)] = \sum_{u\in T}P(Y_{1:|u|}=u). $$

The argument is about prefix probability mass, not specifically diffusion independence. DDTree needs factorization only because that's the only tractable prefix-probability estimate vanilla DFlash gives them.

If DFlash 2 gives us a tractable first-order conditional surrogate, then we can replace the independent distribution by a Markov-style surrogate.

Moreover, if

$$ r_i(v\mid u)\le1, $$

then

$$ Q_{\text{pair}}(uv) = Q_{\text{pair}}(u)r_i(v\mid u) \le Q_{\text{pair}}(u). $$

So an ancestor still has at least as much probability mass as a descendant.

That means DDTree's elegant:

take the top-\(B\) prefixes with a best-first search

structure should survive.

You potentially get a result along the lines of:

DDTree's optimal tree-construction result extends from independent diffusion marginals to any efficiently computable causal surrogate with monotone prefix probabilities. DFlash 2 provides such a surrogate essentially for free.

That is much more interesting than an engineering tweak.

And there's a beautiful experimental question

I wouldn't start by measuring tokens/sec.

I'd start by measuring how wrong DDTree's surrogate is.

For every actual target verification round, record the real target prefix \(y_{1:d}\). Then ask:

At depth \(d\), what rank did the actual target prefix have according to DDTree?

Do this for:

$$ S_{\text{DDTree}}(u) $$

versus

$$ S_{\text{DFlash2-pairwise}}(u). $$

Then plot something like:

Target-prefix coverage vs tree budget

$$ C(B,d) = P(\text{target prefix of depth }d\text{ appears in }T_B). $$

That's almost directly connected to acceptance:

$$ P(\alpha_T\ge d)=C(B,d). $$

Imagine getting a result like:

Scorer	\(B=32\)	\(B=64\)	\(B=128\)
DDTree marginal	51%	63%	72%
DFlash2 pairwise	68%	79%	86%

at, say, depth 6.

Before you've even optimized a kernel, you've demonstrated:

DDTree is spending verification budget on the wrong branches, and DFlash2's pairwise information fixes a meaningful portion of it.

That's a very compelling research result.

And DFlash 2 itself says there's still substantial selection headroom: its learned selector improves acceptance but remains far below its top-16 oracle. The authors explicitly describe pairwise scoring as their simplest selector and say there's plenty left to explore.

This feels unusually well timed.
